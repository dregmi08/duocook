Every order starts a 3,000-step clock. The longer it waits, the smaller the tip.

A cooking task can fail even when every action looks sensible.

A cook chops the right ingredient but blocks the board an urgent order needs. Another waits beside a boiling pot instead of starting the next dish.

Efficient cooking means tracking unfinished work, assigning complementary jobs, and re-planning as equipment frees up. DuoCook tests whether a VLM can do that from visual observations and structured task information, with deadlines running.

20 kitchens

Overcooked-style layouts built in Unreal Engine by coding agents through SimWorld Studio, then refined by hand and checked against an actor contract.

Centralized control

One model sees one shared context and assigns actions to both players. Coordination is the thing under test, not guessing what an independent partner will do.

Scored on the clock

Orders arrive every 1,000 steps and expire after 3,000. Faster delivery earns bigger tips, and consecutive deliveries build a combo.

Five kinds of kitchen, five kinds of conflict.

Each category changes what the two cooks must negotiate: who gets the chopping board, when the floor moves, or how a dish crosses a wall.

What the controller sees

At every decision both players share the same context:

  • A top-down kitchen image with grid labels (cells are named like r3c5) and a prop legend
  • A map-specific system prompt of roughly 4,000 words with rules and recipes
  • Live state: player actions, positions, held items, station claims, orders, and prep status
  • The list of legal operations, and plain feedback when a command is rejected
  • The six most recent responses with accept/reject results (always on)
  • In the memory condition only: a shared notebook of observations the players write themselves

Four VLMs, twenty maps, five numbers.

Every model controls both players through one controller on the same 20 maps. Each episode runs 4,000 steps, and the initial state and order sequence are fixed across models and conditions.

What the memory ablation changes

The memory-enabled condition adds a shared notebook that the two players write themselves. With each decision a player may attach up to three observations (grid cell, object kind, status, optional note). Both players read the table back on every call, with each row marked by its author.

Entries fade with a 600-step half-life, and a partner’s entries fade twice as fast. They show as three bands (just seen, fairly sure, hazy), and the store holds at most 24 entries, 16 per author. Nothing is checked against the simulator, so a stale or wrong row is part of what the condition tests.

Both conditions keep the image, legend, live state, legal operations, failure feedback, and the six-response action history. Only the shared notebook is removed. Across memory-enabled episodes, 40.1% of the entries a player read (10,732 of 26,750) were written by its partner.

How scores are aggregated

Metrics are computed per episode and averaged with equal weight over the 20 maps. Memory effects are paired per-map differences, then averaged. With one run per model, map, and condition, results are descriptive and do not estimate run-to-run variability.

How to read comparisons with other benchmarks

The engine handles navigation, maps carry grid labels, and structured state is supplied as text. Scores here are not directly interchangeable with benchmarks that use different observation and control interfaces.

How the kitchens were built

Two separate claude-opus-5 processes work in sequence. One writes the construction prompt and completion checklist from the game context, a reference image, and the actor naming contract. The other executes that prompt in SimWorld Studio. We then refine decoration, collisions, visibility, and cameras by hand, and run a script that verifies the required actors and their names.

Results

The benchmark is far from solved. The best controller completes about a quarter of the orders, and the rest manage fewer than one in eight.

gpt-6-astra leads clearly

It scores 25.6, more than twice the next model (11.9), and tops every metric except cost. It also costs $5.18 per episode, roughly 19 times what qwen3.8-27b costs.

Cost doesn’t track skill

claude-opus-5 costs almost as much as gpt-6-astra ($4.61) but scores lowest (8.2). The two cheapest models score between those two extremes.

Valid commands aren’t completed orders

claude-opus-5 has accepted commands 52.7% of the time against 32.1% for gemini-3.5-flash-lite, yet gemini scores higher. Both abilities need reporting.

Two categories split the field

Only gpt-6-astra completes any orders on dynamic maps with shared equipment (31.2%). On separated workspaces only it and qwen3.8-27b do (18.8% and 12.5%).

Memory ablation

Adding the shared notebook raises cost for all four models, by about 13% to 67%. Its effect on score is small and mixed, and for most models the interval spans zero. The pattern is not uniform, so we don’t claim memory helps or hurts.

Where DuoCook fits

Overcooked-AI studies cooperation with humans, ProAgent infers teammate intentions, MindAgent’s CuisineWorld supports centralized scheduling, and Collab-Overcooked adds resource isolation and communication. DuoCook adds one visually grounded controller for both players and map variations that expose resource and spatial dependencies.

It evaluates centralized scheduling, not inference about an independent partner. Neither two-cook control nor required handoffs alone is the contribution. Cooking-planning work such as ParaCook and Robotouille, and visual-agent suites such as Balrog, VisualAgentBench, EmbodiedBench, and VideoGameBench, inform the scheduling demands and interface design.
